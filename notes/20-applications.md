# 20 Applications of Integration

`calccode/applications.py` collects the Calc II geometry formulas: arc length, disk and shell volumes, and surface area of revolution. Each one is a single integral, so the whole module is four thin wrappers over `simpson` from `integrals.py`. Arc length and surface area also need f'(x), which comes from `central_diff` in `derivatives.py`. Nothing symbolic happens here; f is a plain Python callable and the answer is a float.

The tests are the interesting part. Arc length of a line segment has to equal the Euclidean distance between its endpoints, and it does to 1e-9 because Simpson is exact on constant integrands. Rotating the semicircle sqrt(r^2 - x^2) about the x-axis should give a sphere: volume 4/3 pi r^3, surface area 4 pi r^2. The volume test is exact to machine precision since f^2 = r^2 - x^2 is a polynomial. I also checked the shell method against the disk method on the region under f(x) = x rotated about the y-axis: shells give 2/3 pi r^3, and the same number falls out of cylinder minus cone, with the cylinder computed by `volume_disk` on a constant function. Two methods, one solid, agreement to 1e-12.

Where this breaks: the endpoint derivative singularity of the semicircle. f'(x) = -x / sqrt(r^2 - x^2) blows up at x = r, and `central_diff` evaluates f at r + h, outside the domain, which is a math domain error for sqrt. I shrank the interval to [-a, a] with a = r - 1e-3 and compared against the closed form for the shrunk arc, 2 r arcsin(a / r). That costs about 2 sqrt(2 r eps) of length at the two missing caps, roughly 0.126 for r = 2, so the full half circumference pi r is only recovered to within that gap. Surface area survives better because the integrand f sqrt(1 + (f')^2) cancels to the constant r analytically, and the numerical derivative is close enough that the cancellation nearly happens in floating point too.

The substitution I kept gesturing at does remove the singularity outright, and it is in the module now. `arc_length_trig_circle(r)` sets x = r sin(t), so dx = r cos(t) dt and the arc length integrand folds to the constant r dt over [-pi/2, pi/2], the same trick that worked for 1/sqrt(x) back in `integrals.py`. Simpson on a constant is exact, so the half circumference comes back as pi r to machine precision at any panel count, with no epsilon and no missing caps. The singularity was never in the curve; it was in the choice of x as the parameter.
