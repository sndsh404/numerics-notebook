# 28 Orthogonalization

Note 09 ended by saying singular values would give a cleaner rank answer, and note 18 ended by pointing at QR. `calccode/orthogonal.py` is the follow-through on both. Gram-Schmidt comes in two flavors that differ by one line: the classical loop projects each new column against the original column, the modified loop projects against the partially cleaned vector it already holds. Same operation count. Wildly different numerics, and the test suite proves it with one matrix.

Householder QR replaces projections with reflections. Each step zeroes everything below the diagonal of one column by reflecting it onto the first axis, and the reflectors are stored as vectors and replayed against identity columns at the end to build Q. The SVD is one-sided Jacobi: Givens rotations applied to column pairs until every pair of columns is orthogonal, at which point the column norms are the singular values and the accumulated rotations are V. Fifty sweep cap, convergence measured by the off-diagonal norm of A^T A. Once you have U, s, and V, the rest of the module is bookkeeping. Pseudoinverse is V S^+ U^T. Condition number is s_max over s_min. Rank is a count above a relative cutoff. Rank-k approximation is a truncated sum, and least squares through QR never squares the condition number the way the normal equations do.

The Hilbert matrix is where it all shows up at once. In 8x8, elimination rank says 8 because eight pivots survive. The singular values run from 1.7 down to 1.1e-10, so `matrix_rank` at tol 1e-8 says 7, and at 1e-4 it says 4. In 10x10 the condition number is 2.1e12, the normal equations in `regression.py` raise "matrix is singular", and `lstsq_qr` recovers the constructed solution to 5e-3.

Where this breaks: classical Gram-Schmidt on a nearly dependent basis. With columns (1, 1e-8, 0, 0) and its two rotations, my test measures an orthonormality error of 7.1e-1 for the classical loop against 1.2e-8 for the modified one, a factor of sixty million for the price of moving one subtraction. The classical Q is not even close to orthogonal. Householder and Jacobi do not have this failure mode, since they only ever apply orthogonal transformations to the data; the lesson generalizes. If your algorithm builds orthogonality by subtraction it will lose it, and if it builds orthogonality by reflection or rotation it will keep it.
