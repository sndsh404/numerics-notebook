# 25 Classical machine learning

`calccode/ml.py` is the classical half of machine learning, everything before neural nets. The perceptron is the oldest item here: one online pass per epoch, and any misclassified point moves the weight vector by `lr * y * x`. On separable data a theorem says it stops, and on my two blobs it does, in a handful of epochs. k-nearest neighbors stores the training set and votes among the k closest points, brute force, no index structures. Gaussian naive Bayes estimates a mean and variance per class per feature and scores new points by summed log-likelihood. Ridge regression lives in `regression.py` because it is the normal equations with `lambda * I` added before the same Gaussian elimination solve.

Two things the tests pinned down. First, tie-breaks matter. k-NN with k = 2 and one neighbor per class is a coin flip unless you write the rule down; mine goes to the lowest label, so predictions never depend on dict ordering. Second, ridge earns its keep exactly where OLS hurts. On a dataset where x2 is x1 plus 1e-4 of noise, X^T X is nearly singular and OLS swings the coefficients to fit training noise. With lambda = 1 the test MSE drops below the OLS value, and at lambda = 0 the ridge solve reproduces the OLS weights to 1e-6, which is the sanity check that the penalty is the only difference.

Where this breaks: k-NN in high dimensions. I drew 200 uniform points in [0, 1]^d and measured pairwise distances. In 2D the spread (max minus min, over the mean) is 2.5. In 20D it falls to 1.1, and in 100D to 0.47. Every point is roughly the same distance from every other point, so "nearest" stops meaning anything and the vote becomes noise. The fix in practice is to reduce dimensions or weight the features, neither of which a brute-force loop can do for you. The perceptron has its own version of this: on non-separable data it never converges at all, and the epoch cap is the only thing standing between you and an infinite loop.
